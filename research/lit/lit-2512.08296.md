## Takeaway
The only equal-budget single-versus-multi comparison I found that includes SWE-bench Verified: every multi-agent topology scored below the single agent on SWE-bench Verified (n=20 subset), on Terminal-Bench, and on the sequential planning task, with overhead of 58% to 515% in turns. Gains appear only on parallelisable tasks with a weak single-agent baseline.

## Method
- One single-agent system (SAS) against four multi-agent topologies: Independent, Centralized (orchestrator plus 3 sub-agents), Decentralized, Hybrid (Appendix E.2: "3 sub-agents with 1 orchestrator"); 180 configurations; models from OpenAI, Google and Anthropic (GPT-5 family, Gemini 2.0/2.5/3, Claude Sonnet 3.7/4/4.5), no open-weight model (§4.1).
- Budget: "All MAS and SAS configurations were matched for total reasoning-token budget (mean 4,800 tokens per trial)" (§4.4); §4.1: MAS got equal compute through parallel processing while SAS got more rounds. The SWE appendix gives iteration caps (SAS "maximum 10 iterations per instance"), not token matching (App. D/E.2): budget matching on the SWE tasks is NOT verified by me.
- SWE-bench Verified: a 20-instance subset (seed 42), 7 tools; role assignment for editing in each topology is not stated (App. D).

## Relevance
Part D and B. For a task where the single agent is near our rate (below 45%), the paper's own regression predicts a gain only if the task is parallelisable, and file edits in one repo are not. All multi-agent costs are turns, which are our binding cap (28 to 45 calls).

Transfers to our constraints: partly. Frontier closed models, n=20 on SWE, no 32k context or wall-clock constraint, topology implementation unknown. The direction of the SWE result matches Cognition's argument; the size of the effect is not estimable (CIs overlap).
